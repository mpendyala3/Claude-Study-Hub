What this exam is actually about It is not a Claude trivia quiz and it is not an engineering exam. It tests whether you can be trusted to use Claude on real business work without supervision — which in practice means three judgements, over and over: is this output good enough to send?, is this the right tool and model for the job?, and am I allowed to put this data in here? The certification's own description names the fourth: recognising your limits and escalating technical work to Architects and Developers.
The single most useful rule for picking answers Almost every distractor on this exam is one of two failures: it trusts the model instead of verifying it, or it asks nicely instead of controlling the input. The official sample items show both — self-reported confidence is not an accuracy signal, and telling Claude not to retain a file is not a substitute for redacting it. When two options both sound responsible, pick the one that changes what goes in or checks what came out against an authoritative source.

Read the weights as a study budget Domain 2 alone is 21% — around 13 items, more than any other domain and half again the size of Domain 1. Domains 2, 4 and 6 together are 52% of the exam: evaluate the output, fit it into a workflow, stay inside policy. If your time is limited, that is where it goes. Domain 7 at 10% is the smallest, but it is also the cheapest to learn — it is largely one diagnostic habit applied repeatedly.

The eight distractor patterns

Nearly every wrong answer on an associate-level exam is one of these dressed in business language. Learn them as a checklist and you can usually eliminate two options before you finish reading the stem.

PatternThe tempting wrong answerWhat the exam wants
Self-reported confidence"Ask Claude how confident it is, and send it if the rating is high"Verification against an authoritative source. A model's stated confidence is generated text, not a measurement
Instruction as a control"Upload the file but tell Claude not to retain it"Change what goes in: redact, anonymise, aggregate, or use an approved workspace. A request is not a control
Cosmetic fix"Reword it to sound more authoritative, then send"Address correctness or completeness. Polish never repairs a factual defect
Abandon the task"Skip the analysis entirely" / "do it all manually instead"The enabling control that lets the work proceed safely. Over-caution is scored wrong just as often as recklessness
Always the biggest model"Use the most capable model for everything to be safe"Match the tier to the task. Deliberately spending the whole budget on easy work is a failure to select
Re-prompting harder"Tell Claude to be more accurate and try again"Supply the missing input — the source document, the Project knowledge, the example — or decompose the request
Re-explaining every chat"Paste the brand guidelines into each new conversation"Persist durable context once, in Project instructions and knowledge, and maintain it
Wrong escalation, either way"Hand it to engineering" for something in-product · "just build it yourself" for an API integrationAssociates own in-product configuration and judgement; architecture, APIs and agentic systems go to Architects and Developers

An eight-day plan

  1. Day 1 — Platform. Read the primer end to end, then open Claude and touch every surface it names: start a Project, add instructions and one knowledge file, generate an artifact, run one Research query, switch models mid-conversation, open an incognito chat. Reading about Projects and having configured one are different kinds of knowledge, and the exam tests the second.
  2. Day 2 — Domain 2 first. It is 21% of the exam. Read it before Domain 1, while you are freshest, and do its Q&A drills twice.
  3. Day 3 — Domains 1 and 7. Prompting and troubleshooting are the same skill viewed from two ends; reading them together makes both shorter.
  4. Day 4 — Domains 3 and 5. Product/model selection and configuration. Do the model-selection and Project-configuration exercises the same day, because these are the two domains where recall beats reasoning.
  5. Day 5 — Domains 4 and 6. Workflow design and governance, together 31% of the exam. Both are judgement domains: pay attention to the decision tables, not the prose.
  6. Day 6 — Build. Work every exercise without the reference solution open. Get to a partial score, read which checks failed, fix them. That loop is what transfers.
  7. Day 7 — Sit it timed. Take the mock exam in Exam mode: 120 minutes, no feedback until you submit. This bank is harder than the real exam, so treat 780+ as a green light and anything under 700 as a signal to go back rather than book.
  8. Day 8 — Review, then re-take shuffled. Read every explanation, including on items you got right — the "why the others are ruled out" text is where the marginal points live. Then retake with the order shuffled, to check you learned the principle rather than the position.

One scoring detail that changes how you pace Your pass/fail is decided by the total scaled score only. The per-domain percentages on your report are diagnostic — there is no per-domain minimum to clear. So there is no reason to over-invest in a domain you find hard at the cost of losing easy marks elsewhere: at roughly two minutes per item, an item you cannot resolve in ninety seconds is worth flagging and leaving. Every item is worth the same.
