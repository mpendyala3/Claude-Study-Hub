What this exam is actually about Almost every item hands you a production system, a measured symptom and a constraint that rules out the obvious fix, and asks for the change you would defend. The constraint is the whole item: a latency SLA, a regulated data class, a fixed budget, an auditor, a stakeholder who has already been promised something. An option that is excellent engineering and violates the stated constraint is wrong, every time. Read the constraint before you read the options, and the field usually halves.
The three shapes almost every distractor takes It solves a different problem than the one measured — tuning the prompt when the evidence points at retrieval, upgrading the model when the evidence points at context. It buys a control that detects instead of one that prevents — logging, alerting, confirmation dialogs and after-the-fact audits standing in for removing a capability that was never needed. Or it is an engineering answer to a stakeholder question — more accuracy offered to a sponsor who asked what the thing will cost, a benchmark quoted to a compliance officer who asked where the data goes.

Read the weights as a study budget Integration at 19% is the single largest domain and it also has the most objectives — eight, against five or six everywhere else — so each one is thinner but none is safe. Integration plus Solution Design plus Evaluation is 52% of the paper. The two domains most engineers under-prepare are the two that together are 28%: Governance and Stakeholder Communication. If you come from a delivery background you will find Domain 6 easier than the blueprint implies; if you come from a pure engineering background, budget real time for it, because “explain this trade-off to the CFO” is a skill with its own wrong answers.

The three official sample items, worked

The guide publishes three sample items with an answer key. They are worth more than their length suggests, because they fix the cognitive level of the paper: none of the three is a recall question, and all three turn on eliminating options that are individually reasonable. Read them as a calibration exercise.

ItemWhat it is really testing
Sample 1
Domain 3 — least privilege		 A support agent holds refund and delete-account tools that support staff never use. The credited answer is to remove them from the configuration. Logging makes misuse auditable after it happens and a confirmation prompt asks the model to police itself; both are detective or compensating controls standing where a preventive one belongs. A bigger model is not an authorisation control at all. The generalisation to carry into the exam: when a capability is not needed by the role, the answer is almost always to remove it, not to watch it. See 3.1 and 3.2.
Sample 2
Domain 2 — caching and ordering		 An 8,000-token static prefix is resent on every request; both latency and cost are concerns. The credited answer puts the stable content first and enables prompt caching, which is the only option that improves both without discarding required context. Truncation loses policy the system needs; downsizing the model blindly trades quality for the wrong saving; moving the policy into a few-shot block changes where the text sits without making any prefix reusable. The generalisation: when an option improves one metric by sacrificing a stated requirement, it loses to one that improves both. See 2.5 and 4.5.
Sample 3
Domain 4 — diagnosis		 A RAG system starts returning confident wrong answers after a document refresh, with latency and model version unchanged. The credited answer is the retrieval and indexing step. Every other option names something the refresh could not have changed. The generalisation, and it is the single most reusable habit on this exam: find the thing that changed, and start there. Confident and wrong points at context, not at the model. See 4.4 and 3.5.
What the samples tell you about the distractors In all three, every wrong option is something a competent engineer might say in a meeting. None is absurd. That is the difficulty model of this exam: you are not eliminating nonsense, you are ranking four defensible actions against a constraint the stem stated once. If you find yourself thinking “honestly you would do all four”, go back and re-read the sentence that names the measurement or the limit — that sentence is the discriminator.

The constraint is the tie-breaker Thornbury forbids PHI leaving the boundary, so an option that improves accuracy by calling an external enrichment service is wrong there and right at Merrowfield. Calderon has a 3-second p95 in a contract, so an option that adds a verification pass is wrong there and right at Thornbury, where a clinician is already in the loop. Merrowfield is cost-bounded, so an option that doubles per-item spend to gain two points of accuracy is wrong there and defensible at Aldergate. Nothing on this exam is universally correct.

How to use that table Read it once now as a self-assessment: mark every objective you could not explain to a colleague for two minutes without notes. That marked set, not the domain weights, is your study plan — the weights tell you what the exam asks about, but your marks tell you where you will lose points. Come back to it after the mock exam and compare: the objectives you failed on the mock but did not mark here are the dangerous ones, because you did not know you did not know them.

The distractor patterns this exam reuses

Professional-level items rarely contain a wrong answer. They contain four defensible answers, three of which are defensible somewhere else. These are the sixteen shapes that recur; being able to name one is worth more under time pressure than re-reading the stem a third time.

PatternHow it looks, and the tell
Detective control for a preventive requirementLogging, alerting, an audit trail or a confirmation prompt offered where the requirement is that the thing must not happen. Tell: the option describes what you will know afterwards. If the stem says “must never”, the answer removes the capability or blocks it in code.
Model as the enforcer“Instruct the model to always verify…”, “add to the system prompt that it must never…”. Tell: the guarantee depends on the probabilistic component. Absolute requirements live outside the model.
Bigger model as the fixOffered for a failure caused by missing context, wrong retrieval, an absent tool or an undefined term. Tell: the stem already told you the model has never seen the information it needed.
Solves a different symptomThe stem measures cost; the option improves accuracy. The stem measures p95; the option improves throughput. Tell: read the last sentence again and check the option moves the metric it names.
Ignores the stated constraintExcellent engineering that crosses a regulatory boundary, exceeds a contractual SLA, or spends past a stated budget. Tell: the constraint sentence is usually mid-stem and unrepeated. It is not decoration.
Optimises before measuringA concrete optimisation offered where nothing has yet established what is slow, expensive or wrong. Tell: the stem contains no baseline. The credited answer usually establishes one.
Single aggregate metricOne overall accuracy or satisfaction number proposed as the success criterion. Tell: the stem mentions a segment, a subgroup or a tail. Aggregates hide exactly those.
Under-powered experimentAn A/B test with a sample that could not detect the effect, no pre-declared metric, or two variables changed at once. Tell: the option promises a conclusion the design cannot support.
Human in the loop as decorationA reviewer placed where they see everything and therefore attend to nothing, or after the irreversible action. Tell: ask what the human would have to do to catch the failure, and whether the volume makes that possible.
More context instead of better contextRaising the window, loading the whole corpus, appending everything “just in case”. Tell: capacity is not attention, and the relevant fact was already in the window in the failing case.
Vector search for a lookup questionSemantic retrieval offered where users ask for a specific record, an exact identifier or a numeric filter. Tell: the query pattern in the stem is structured. A query beats an embedding there.
Protocol chosen by prestigeMCP for a single point-to-point call, or a bespoke integration where a reusable server already exists. Tell: count the consumers. One consumer and one call rarely justifies a protocol surface.
Multi-agent for a linear problemParallel agents proposed for work whose steps are ordered and dependent. Tell: if step two needs step one's output, the parallelism buys coordination cost and nothing else.
Engineering answer to a stakeholder questionA sponsor asks about cost or timeline; the option answers with architecture or benchmark scores. Tell: identify who is asking and in what unit they will be held accountable.
Promising a guaranteeAn SLA on accuracy, a commitment that the system will never hallucinate, a fixed answer to “how often will it be wrong” without a measurement plan. Tell: the credited answer commits to a measured range with a review cadence, not to certainty.
Deferring the hard decision“Escalate to leadership”, “gather more requirements”, “convene a working group” where the stem has already given you enough to decide. Tell: the option produces no change to the system. Sometimes it is right — but only when the stem shows a genuine conflict of authority, not just difficulty.

Where the path stops and this hub starts The learning path teaches the concepts. Two things about the scoring that change how you sit it The score is scaled and criterion-referenced: 720 is a fixed standard, not a percentile, so you are not competing against other candidates and there is no curve to hope for. And there is no penalty for a wrong answer — the scaled score is a function of items correct. Never leave an item blank. With 120 minutes for 63 items you have just under two minutes each: if an item has held you for three, mark your best elimination, flag it, and move on. Coming back with a fresh read is worth more than grinding.

If you are also taking CCAR-F
